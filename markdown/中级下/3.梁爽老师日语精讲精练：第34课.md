Grammar Notes
1. The passive form of verbs
The passive form of Japanese verbs is formed by attaching “れる” or “られる” to the verb stem. The specific conjuga-
tion rules are as follows:
Group I (godan) verbs:
Change the final u-row kana of the verb to the corresponding a-row kana and attach “れる”.
Note: verbs ending in “う” first change to “わ” before attaching “れる”. For example:
かえ かえ
返す：かえす→さ＋れる＝返される
か か
書く：かく→か＋れる＝書かれる
う う
打つ：うつ→た＋れる＝打たれる
よ よ
読む：よむ→ま＋れる＝読まれる
よ よ
呼ぶ：よぶ→ば＋れる＝呼ばれる
おく おく
送る：おくる→ら＋れる＝送られる
つか つか
使う：つかう→わ＋れる＝使われる
Group II (ichidan) verbs:
Remove the final kana “る” of the verb and attach “られる”. For example:
あつ あつ
集める：あつめる＋られる＝集められる
み み
見る：みる＋られる＝見られる
Group III (irregular) verbs:
(1) Suru-verbs: change “する” to “される”. That is:

2. Japanese passive sentences I: direct passive sentences <basic passive sentences A, B>
In Japanese, a sentence whose subject is the agent of the action is called an “active sentence”, and one whose subject is the receiver of the
う みぶん
action is called a “passive sentence” (Japanese: 受け身文). The correspondence between Japanese “active sentences” and “passive sentences”

3. Japanese passive sentences II: possessor passive sentences
The possessor passive is mainly used to describe that something belonging to the receiver of the action, or a part of their body, is affected by some ac-
tion. Possessor passive sentences often carry a clear sense of being adversely affected. The basic pattern is:
Possessor passive: N が/は N に N を V（ら）れる
Here, N is the possessor and also the receiver of the action; it serves as the subject of the possessor passive, marked with “が/は”;
N is N's possession or a part of the body — the direct object of the predicate verb, marked with “を”;
the agent N is marked with “に”.
わたし でんしゃ なか あし ふ
① （私は）電車の中で足を踏まれました。／I had my foot stepped on in the train.
わたし ち かてつ さいふ ぬす
② （私は）パリの地下鉄で（すりに）財布を盗まれました。／In the Paris
metro, I had my wallet stolen (by a pickpocket).
と いもうと た
③ 取っておいたケーキを 妹 に食べられてしまった。／The cake I had saved was eaten by my younger sister.
The Japanese possessor passive differs slightly from the Chinese passive in expression, so special attention is needed here.
In Japanese, the possessor and the possession (or body part) serve as the subject (which can be omitted) and the object of the
verb respectively; it cannot be expressed in the manner of example (4-b).

4. Japanese passive sentences III: indirect passive sentences
The “indirect passive” refers to cases where the action does not directly affect the subject (mostly first person), but
the subject suffers or is troubled by some event or someone else's action — an indirect effect; some books also call it the “adversative
passive”. Since predicate verbs can be divided into intransitive and transitive verbs, the “indirect passive” has the following
two patterns:
Indirect passive (intransitive verb): N が/は N に V（ら）れる
Indirect passive (transitive verb): N が/は N に N を V（ら）れる
Here, N is the adversely affected party, serving as subject marked with “が/は”; N is the actor, marked with the particle “に”.
The indirect passive expresses that N suffers an adverse ef-
fect or damage due to some unintentional action of N. When N's action is a transitive verb, its object N is marked with “を”.

② 遠足の日に雨に降られて、びしょびしょでした。／On the day of the excursion I got caught in the rain and

5. Japanese passive sentences IV: non-emotive passive sentences
The Japanese “non-emotive passive” is closest in usage to the English passive. This is mainly due to the influence of the mass
translation and introduction of European and American literary works starting in the Meiji period, which gradually

<!-- Page 12 -->

popularized the use of the “non-emotive passive”.
The “non-emotive passive” is often used to state well-known social phenomena, with a thing or matter as the subject.
Depending on whether the agent of the action needs to be explicitly stated, the “non-emotive passive” can be divided into two types:
Non-emotive passive A (unspecified agent): N が/は（N に）V（ら）れる
Whether or not the agent (the doer of the action) is a specific person, the key fea-
ture of the “non-emotive passive” is that the subject N must be a “thing or matter”, marked with “が” or “は”. The agent is marked with “に”,
and since the agent is not a specific person, “N に” is generally omitted.
きょうかい ねんまえ た
① この教会は600年前に建てられました。／This church was built 600 years ago.
にほんご おお ひと まな
② 日本語は多くの人に学ばれています。／Japanese is studied by many people.
じしん おお かおく こわ
③ 地震で、多くの家屋が壊されました。／Many houses were destroyed in the earthquake.
Non-emotive passive B (specified agent): N が/は N によって V（ら）れる
The “non-emotive passive B” is mainly used to describe inventions, creations, or new discoveries. In this case the
agent (doer of the action) N must be explicitly stated and marked with “によって”.
じん にほん つた
④ タバコはポルトガル人によって、日本に伝えられました。／Tobacco was introduced
to Japan by the Portuguese.
なつめそうせき か めいしょうせつ
⑤ これは夏目漱石によって書かれた名小説です。／This is a famous novel written
by Natsume Soseki.
とじま しんかいまことかんとく つく

10. V るものではない ＜advice; warning＞
The dictionary form of a verb followed by “ものではない” is used to give advice, meaning “one should not (do) ...”.

11. V るには ＜purpose＞
The dictionary form of a verb followed by “には” expresses a compound concept of purpose and condition. “V るには” is
basically synonymous with “V るために”, expressing purpose. The second clause after “V るには” often expresses a necessary
condition, the means or method required to achieve the purpose of the first clause, or the effort that must be made.
