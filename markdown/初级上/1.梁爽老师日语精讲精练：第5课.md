Grammar Notes
1. N は N です。 <noun-predicate sentence>
「は」 is the “topic particle” in noun-predicate sentences, used to mark the subject/topic of the sentence; it is read “wa”.
Because 「は」 marks the topic, it usually has no direct English translation.
The sentence-final 「です」 is the “copula”, equivalent to “am/is/are”, much like the verb “be” in English. At the same time, ending a sentence with 「です」 also conveys the speaker's polite tone toward the listener.

わたし やまだ
① 私は山田です。／I'm Yamada.
り ちゅうごくじん
② 李さんは中国人です。／Li is Chinese.
なかむら にほんじん
③ 中村さんは日本人です。／Nakamura is Japanese.
2. N は N ではありません。 <negative form of the noun-predicate sentence>
「ではありません」 is the negative form of 「です」, equivalent to “is not”. The 「では
in 「ではありません」 is mostly used in formal settings such as speeches or in writing; its colloquial form is
「じゃ」。
わたし がくせい
① 私は学生ではありません。／I am not a student.
さとう かいしゃいん
② 佐藤さんは会社員ではありません。／Sato is not a company employee.
じん
③ ジョージさんはイギリス人じゃありません。／George is not British.

3. Sentence + か。 <question>
The particle 「か」 attached to the end of a declarative sentence forms a question, like the question mark in English. In writing,
Japanese questions generally do not use “?” but end with “。” instead. 「か」 can be attached to a declarative sentence
to form a yes-no question; such sentences are usually answered with 「はい」 or 「いいえ」.

A question word can also be added to form a wh-question; the respondent answers directly according to the meaning of the question word.

4. N で、N です。 <noun conjunction (continuative form)>
“で” is the continuative form of “です”, used to connect noun sentences. In this lesson, when two noun-predicate
sentences share the same subject, they can be joined into one sentence with “で”, and the subject of the second clause can be omitted. Here,
the two clauses are in a sequential relationship. Between Japanese clauses, the comma “、” is generally used
rather than “，”.

er.

6. Sentence + ね。 <confirmation (sentence-final particle ね-1)>
The particle “ね” attached to the end of a sentence expresses the tone of “seeking confirmation” from the listener. It is one of the more
commonly used “sentence-final particles” in Japanese — particles used at the end of a sentence. When “ね” expresses “confirmation”,
it is read with rising intonation and a crisp feel.

Expression Expansion
1. Subjects in Japanese
Compared with Chinese or English, Japanese usually omits subjects such as “I”, “you”, and “he/she”. For ex-
example:
A: Are you Yamada?
B: Yes, I'm Yamada.
In Japanese, this would more often be expressed as:
やまだ
（◯）A: 山田さんですか。
やまだ
B: はい、山田です。
It would generally not be said this way:
やまだ
（△）A: あなたは山田さんですか。
わたし
B: はい、私は山田です。

2. Useful expressions for first meetings in this lesson
(1) When you take the initiative to approach and speak to someone
When you take the initiative to approach someone, you can use “あのう” followed by what you want to say.
“あのう” is a filler word Japanese people often use, equivalent to “um...”. When talking with strang-
ers, you can start with “あのう” to avoid sounding abrupt. But it is not recommended to overuse it, or you will
sound long-winded.
ちゅうごく かた
① A: あのう、中国の方ですか。／Excuse me, are you Chinese?

(2) The “three-step self-introduction” for first meetings
When meeting someone for the first time, you should first introduce yourself. A simple self-introduction in Japanese
can follow this order:
Step 1: greeting
Step 2: introduce who you are
Step 3: exchange pleasantries
Step 1: greeting
A self-introduction is usually given to someone you don't know, or in fairly formal settings,
so for the greeting part you can choose “おはようございます”,
“こんにちは”, or “こんばんは” depending on the time of day.
はじ はじ
In addition, add “初めまして”. “初めまして” is the greeting for a first meeting, with the
meaning “nice to meet you (please treat me well)”.
Step 2: introduce who you are
しゃ

ちょう ぶちょう
長” or “部長” to yourself. You can use the following sentences to introduce yourself; sentence ② is more po-
lite and humble than ①.

① （私は）〇〇です。／I'm ____.
わたし もう
② （私は）〇〇と申します。／My name is ____. (humble)
You can also briefly introduce basic facts about yourself as appropriate, such as nationality, age, etc. For example:

(3) Introducing a third party
しょうかい
When introducing a third party to someone, you can first say “紹介します”, then give the specific introduction.
しょうかい
“紹介します” is a verb sentence meaning “let me introduce”. Japanese often omits the subject; the same is true in verb
わたし しょうかい
sentences — here the subject “私は” is omitted, and the sentence consists only of the verb “紹介します”.

しょうかい

is) George.
